Devotionals · Faith in our own language

Five Filipino words, redeemed in Christ.

These are the same heart-words we wrestle with at fellowship — Loob, Kapwa, Malasakit, Bahala Na, Utang na Loob. Here they slow down into short devotional reads: a verse, a reflection, and a prayer to carry into your week.

Introduction

We're Filipino young pros — nurses, devs, teachers, freelancers, fresh grads — learning to follow Jesus right where we are. And we've found that the gospel doesn't erase our language; it fills it. The values our lola taught us, the ones baked into how we love and how we work, were never meant to be left at the church door. Christ meets them, heals what's broken in them, and points them home.

So take these slow. One read, one verse, one short prayer at a time — sa umaga before the commute, or sa gabi when the day finally quiets down. Let the Word do what only the Word can do: reshape the loob.

The five devotional reads

Devotional 01

Loob

the inner self, the will

“The Lord sees not as man sees: man looks on the outward appearance, but the Lord looks on the heart.”

1 Samuel 16:7 ESV

Loob is the inside of you — your hidden self, your will, what's really going on behind the “okay lang ako.” We're so good at managing the labas: the polished resume, the curated feed, the “I'm fine” at standup. But God isn't impressed by the surface, and He isn't fooled by it either. When Samuel was sizing up the tall, good-looking candidates, God quietly passed over every one of them and chose the boy nobody called in from the field. He was reading a different page — the loob.

That's terrifying and freeing at the same time. Terrifying, because He sees the parts we hide. Freeing, because it means we can finally stop performing. You don't have to keep your loob spotless to be loved here; you have to bring it. Sabihin mo na lang sa Kanya — the envy, the burnout, the ambition that's quietly become an idol. He already knows. He's just waiting for you to stop pretending so He can start healing.

And He does heal it. The same God who looks on the heart is the God who promised to give us a new one — to write His Word not on a wall but on the inside, where it actually changes us. Before He upgrades your career, let Him renew your loob. Who you are in the unseen is the real you He's building.

Prayer Lord, You see my loob — the parts I hide from everyone else. Search it, soften it, and make the inside of me match the calling You've placed on my life. Amen.

Devotional 02

Kapwa

shared humanity, one-with-the-other

“Do nothing from selfish ambition or conceit, but in humility count others more significant than yourselves. Let each of you look not only to his own interests, but also to the interests of others.”

Philippians 2:3–4 ESV

Kapwa is one of the most beautiful words we have. It doesn't just mean “other person” — it means the person who shares your humanity, the one you recognize yourself in. When you treat someone as kapwa, the wall between “me” and “you” gets thin. You feel their hunger as a version of your own. That instinct isn't just Filipino culture; it's a fingerprint of the God who made us in His image, all of us, no exceptions.

Here's where the gospel deepens it. At the foot of the cross, the ground is level. The boss and the messenger, the topnotcher and the one still job-hunting, the put-together and the barely-holding-on — we are all equally in need of grace, and all equally offered it. Paul says to count others more significant than yourself. That's not low self-esteem; it's the math of a person so secure in Christ's love that they can spend their energy noticing somebody else.

So pakikipagkapwa becomes worship. The new officemate eating alone. The kuya guard you walk past every day. The friend who's gone quiet online. To see them as kapwa — truly see them — and to move toward them is to love the way Jesus loved us first, when we were strangers and He made us family.

Prayer Jesus, You crossed every distance to call me Yours. Give me eyes to see each person as kapwa, and a heart secure enough to put them first. Amen.

Devotional 03

Malasakit

compassionate care

“When he saw the crowds, he had compassion for them, because they were harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd.”

Matthew 9:36 ESV

Malasakit is care that costs you something. It's the difference between “sayang naman” said from a distance and the friend who actually shows up — who carries part of the weight as if it were their own. It's love that refuses to look away. And that is exactly the word the Gospels reach for when they describe Jesus looking at a crowd. He didn't see a problem to manage; He saw tired, scattered people, and something moved in Him.

The Greek word there is visceral — it means He felt it in His gut. The Lord of glory was not professionally polite about our suffering; He was wrecked by it, enough to do something about it that cost Him everything. The cross is malasakit taken to its furthest end: He entered our helplessness and carried what we could never carry ourselves.

Which means our malasakit isn't something we manufacture by trying harder to be nice. It overflows from being on the receiving end of His. We notice the burned-out workmate because Someone first noticed us. We carry a friend's burden because Christ first carried ours all the way to Calvary. Today, let His compassion become contagious through you — one small, costly act of showing up.

Prayer Father, You had malasakit on me when I was helpless. Let that same compassion move me toward the tired and the unseen around me today. Amen.

Devotional 04

Bahala Na

faith-filled trust in God

“Trust in the Lord with all your heart, and do not lean on your own understanding. In all your ways acknowledge him, and he will make straight your paths.”

Proverbs 3:5–6 ESV

“Bahala na” gets a bad rap — like it's just shrugging and leaving everything to luck. But listen to the older heart of the phrase: many trace it to “Bathala na” — God will take care of it. That's not fatalism. That's faith. The difference is everything: fatalism gives up because no one's in control; faith lets go because Someone is.

Young-pro life hands you a hundred things you can't control. The job offer that's still “under review.” The result that's out of your hands. The future you can't see past this quarter. Proverbs doesn't tell you to stop thinking — it tells you to stop leaning your whole weight on your own understanding, as if your spreadsheet could hold up your life. Do your part fully, with excellence, as unto the Lord. Then release the outcome to the God who actually holds it.

That's the redeemed bahala na: not lazy surrender, but restful trust. You prepare like it depends on you and you rest like it depends on Him — because it does. He has never once lost control of a single life entrusted to Him, and He isn't about to start with yours. You can breathe. Bathala na — and He is good.

Prayer Lord, I've done my part — now I entrust the rest to You. Bathala na: take care of what I cannot control, and help me rest in Your faithful hands. Amen.

Devotional 05

Utang na Loob

deep gratitude, a debt of the heart

“You are not your own, for you were bought with a price. So glorify God in your body.”

1 Corinthians 6:19–20 ESV

Utang na loob is a debt of the heart — not the kind you pay off at the bank, but the kind you carry because someone did something for you that you could never fully repay. Filipinos understand this deeply: when somebody saves you, you remember. You honor it. Your whole life bends a little toward gratitude. And Scripture says that is exactly the right response to what Jesus has done — only the debt is infinite.

“You were bought with a price.” Not with pesos, not with effort, but with the blood of Christ. We were sold under sin, unable to buy ourselves back, and He paid in full what we owed. That is the ultimate utang na loob — a debt we could never settle, settled forever by Someone else.

But here's the gospel turn: He doesn't hold it over you. Christ doesn't keep score or make you grovel to work it off. The debt is already marked paid. So our gratitude isn't anxious repayment — it's glad, lifelong thanksgiving. We glorify God in our bodies, our jobs, our friendships, our money, not to earn what's already ours, but because we're loved that much. Let your whole life say salamat to the One who paid it all.

Prayer Jesus, You paid a debt I could never repay and called it finished. Let my whole life be a glad salamat — Yours in body, work, and heart. Amen.

Carry it into the week.

These aren't just nice ideas — they're the topics we actually open up about at fellowship, over kape and kwentuhan. If something here stirred your loob, don't carry it alone. There's a seat saved for you, and a family genuinely glad you came.
